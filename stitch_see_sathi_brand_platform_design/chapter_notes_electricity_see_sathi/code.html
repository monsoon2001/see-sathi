Electricity: Ohm's Law & Circuit Dynamics

Master the foundational mechanics of potential difference, directional charge drift, and circuit load resistance. This module directly addresses questions frequently assessed in Group B (Short Answer - 2 Marks) and Group C (Numerical & Diagrammatic Deduction - 3 Marks) of the National Examination Board (NEB/CDC) secondary assessment.

01. What is Electric Current?

In solid conductors such as metallic copper or aluminum wires, valence electrons are loosely bounded to their parent atomic nuclei. In the absence of an applied external electrical field, these free electrons move erratically in random directions due to thermal excitation, colliding continuously with lattice ions. The net movement of charge along any specific cross-sectional plane remains strictly zero.

However, once an electro-chemical cell or battery is established across the terminal extremities of the conductor, an electrostatic potential gradient comes into existence. This potential difference drives a coherent collective drift of free charges. Electric current (I) is rigorously defined as the rate of flow of electric charge through any cross-sectional area of a conductor over unit time:

1 Ampere (A) = 1 Coulomb (C) / 1 Second (s)

03. Ohm's Law: Statement & Verification

Formulated in 1827 by German physicist Georg Simon Ohm, this fundamental physical law describes the linear proportionality between potential drop and steady current through a metallic conductor under invariant ambient constraints.

“At constant physical conditions (such as temperature, strain, and mechanical dimensions), the electric current flowing through a metallic conductor is directly proportional to the potential difference applied across its two terminals.”

Mathematically, if V represents the potential difference and I the current:
V ∝ I  (when Temperature is constant) ⇒ V = I × R, where R is the constant of proportionality known as electrical resistance.

04. Solved Numerical with CDC Rubric

SEE Past Question · 3 Marks
quizQuestion (SEE 2079 Bagmati Province):

An electric heating coil with an internal resistance of 44 Ω is connected across a standard domestic power outlet of 220 V. Calculate the electrical current drawn by the coil and compute the electrical power consumed.

Official CDC Step-by-Step Marking SchemeTotal: 3.0 / 3.0
1
Step 1: Stating Given Parameters & Required Units[0.5 Mark]
Given:
Resistance (R) = 44 Ω
Potential Difference (V) = 220 V
Current (I) = ?
Power (P) = ?
2
Step 2: Applying Ohm's Law for Current[1.0 Mark]

According to Ohm's Law: V = I × R ⇒ I = V / R

Substituting values: I = 220 / 44 = 5 A.

3
Step 3: Calculating Power & Final Conclusion[1.5 Marks]

Electric Power consumed: P = V × I

P = 220 × 5 = 1100 W (or 1.1 kW)

check_circleConclusion: The current drawn is 5 Amperes and the power consumption is 1100 Watts.

05. Summary & High-Yield Examination Points

check

Temperature Dependence

Ohm's law holds true strictly when temperature remains constant. If a filament heats up significantly, its resistance rises, and the V-I curve deviates from linearity.

check

1 Ampere Definition (Frequent 1-Mark Question)

Defined as the current flowing when one Coulomb of charge passes across a specific cross-section per second (1 A = 1 C / 1 s).

check

Ammeter vs Voltmeter Connection

Ammeters have near-zero resistance and must always be wired in series. Voltmeters carry extremely high resistance and must strictly be connected across components in parallel.

check

Ohmic vs Non-Ohmic Conductors

Copper and silver follow linear Ohm's characteristics. Semiconductor diodes, vacuum tubes, and torch bulbs represent non-ohmic devices where resistance varies with voltage.
